# Edge decay and the question of the accessible edge: An evidence-based review

## TL;DR
- The claim "Edges decay primarily due to arbitrage capital" is only **partially confirmed**: The literature is divided - McLean/Pontiff (2016) attribute around half of the decay to the arbitrage/publication effect, but Chen/Zimmermann (2020) and Jensen/Kelly/Pedersen (2023) show that overfitting/publication bias is smaller than often claimed and factors are real; Direct crowding measurements (Lou/Polk Comomentum) exist but are limited to a few factors.
- The "capacity-constrained niche" is real, but largely a **cost-hidden illusion**: anomalies persist longer in illiquid/micro-cap stocks (McLean/Pontiff), but disappear almost completely after realistic trading costs (Chen/Velikov 2023: average ~4 bp/month net; Novy-Marx/Velikov; Patton/Weller).
- For a single retail operator with a small account, the base rate of finding a repeatable net edge after costs is **low**: only about 1 in 5 day traders make any net profit at all in a 6-month window, and only a small minority are consistently profitable (Barber/Lee/Liu/Odean, Taiwan). The most evidence-based realistic sources are event-driven or forced-selling situations with a capacity constraint (merger arbitrage, odd-lot tenders, appraisal proceedings), the volatility risk premium (as a seller, with real tail risk), and structural/tax arbitrage—not classic signal anomalies.

## Key Findings

**On Part 1 (Fact Check):**
1. The decay is empirically robustly documented (~26% OOS, ~58% post-publication by McLean/Pontiff; ~43% by Falck/Rej/Thesmar; ~50% replicated many times), but the *causal attribution* to arbitrage vs. overfitting is the real controversy.
2. Chen/Zimmermann estimate the pure publication bias to be only ~12.3%, which suggests mispricing (i.e. arbitrage-correctable); Jensen/Kelly/Pedersen refute the “replication crisis”.
3. Direct crowding evidence exists (comomentum, short interest, hedge fund AUM), but is factor specific and not universal evidence.
4. Micro-cap persistence is real, but net returns after costs are close to zero - the niche is uninteresting for institutions, but also usually not profitable for retailers.
5. Forced flow effects (fire sales, rating forced sales) continue to exist; The S&P 500 addition effect, on the other hand, has effectively disappeared.
6. The Sharpe standard error formula is correct (Lo 2002), which essentially confirms numerical examples.
7. The DSR criticism is partly justified, but there are alternatives (Harvey/Liu, Romano-Wolf, PBO/CSCV).

**For Part 2 (Accessible Edge):** Ranking according to strength of evidence below.

## Details

### PART 1 — Fact Checking

**A) “Edges decay primarily because arbitrage capital flows into them” — PARTIALLY CONFIRMED / DISPUTED**

The *existence* of decay is well established. McLean & Pontiff (2016, *Journal of Finance* 71(1):5–32) examine 97 predictors; verbatim: “Portfolio returns are 26% lower out-of-sample and 58% lower post-publication. The out-of-sample decline is an upper bound estimate of data mining effects. We estimate a 32% (58% − 26%) lower return from publication-informed trading.” Their interpretation is that the OOS decrease (26%) is an upper limit for statistical/data-mining effects; they attribute the additional post-publication decrease (32%) to publication-informed trading (arbitrage). They also find that post-publication predictor portfolios have higher correlations with other published predictors (consistent with crowding) and that returns remain higher for stocks with high idiosyncratic risk and low liquidity.

Falck, Rej & Thesmar (arXiv 2105.01380) confirm the pattern for 72 factors: OOS-Sharpe ≈ 0.57 × IS-Sharpe (i.e. −43%, median “discount ratio” 0.55) — close to “division by 2”. Chordia/Subrahmanyam/Tong (2014, JAE 58(1):41–58) show that anomaly returns were "approximately halved" after decimalization and link this to hedge fund AUM, short interest and turnover as arbitrage proxies.

**The opposing position (overfitting/selection):** Chen & Zimmermann (2020, Review of Asset Pricing Studies 10(2):249–289, "Publication Bias and the Cross-Section of Stock Returns") develop a publication bias estimator and find that bias-adjusted returns are only 12.3% (SE 1.7 pp) below the in-sample returns — significantly smaller than the ~58 % post-publication decay, which they see as evidence of mispricing (instead of data mining). Their Open Source Asset Pricing project replicates ~98% of clearly significant predictors (t > 1.96). Chen (2021, "The Limits of p-Hacking") argues that the observed distribution of t-values ​​is too wide to be explained by p-hacking alone. Jensen, Kelly & Pedersen (2023, JF 78(5):2465–2518, "Is There a Replication Crisis in Finance?") develop a Bayesian factor model and conclude that the majority of factors can be replicated, can be clustered into 13 topics, works out-of-sample in 93 countries and that the evidence is *strengthened* by the large number of factors. This goes against Hou/Xue/Zhang (2020, RFS 33(5)), who consider large parts of the anomalies to be non-replicable when many microcaps are equalized, and Harvey/Liu/Zhu (2016, RFS 29(1)), who require a t-hurdle of ~3.0 due to multiple testing.

**Direct Crowding Measures:** Lou & Polk (Comomentum, RFS 2022, 35(7):3272-3302) measure abnormal return correlation within momentum portfolios as an arbitrage capital proxy; When co-momentum is high, momentum returns revert strongly (overcrowding); when co-momentum is low, they have a stabilizing effect. Huang/Liu/Lou/Polk (“The Booms and Busts of Beta Arbitrage”, Management Science 2024, 70(8):5367–5385) show the same for BAB. Hanson & Sunderam (2014, “The Growth and Limits of Arbitrage”) use short interest as an arbitrage capital proxy. This evidence is strong but factor specific — it proves crowding for momentum/BAB, not universal.

**Conclusion A:** The *primary* claim is overstated. The consensus is more like this: decay is real and substantial, but it is a *mixture* of (a) statistical exaggeration/publication selection, (b) real arbitrage/crowding, and (c) falling trading costs/increasing liquidity. No reputable paper supports “primarily arbitrage capital” as the sole explanation; McLean/Pontiff themselves split it about 50/50. Noteworthy: Jacobs & Müller (2020, JFE, “Anomalies across the globe”) find that the USA is the *only* country with reliable post-publication declines, which suggests a US-specific arbitrage/attention effect rather than global data mining.

**B) “Capacity-limited niches do not collapse because they are uninteresting for institutions” — PARTIALLY CONFIRMED, but the usable net edge is mostly an illusion**

The persistence thesis has support: McLean/Pontiff find lower decay in illiquid/idiosyncratically risky stocks. But the crucial counter-evidence concerns *net* returns:
- Novy-Marx & Velikov (2016, RFS 29(1):104–147): Anomalies with one-sided monthly turnover < 50% usually still generate significant net spreads (with cost mitigation via buy/hold spread); There are hardly any anomalies with high turnover.
- Chen & Velikov (2023, JFQA 58(3):968–1004, “Zeroing in on the Expected Returns of Anomalies”): Over 204 anomalies the following applies literally: “Net of these effects, the average anomaly's expected return is a moderately 4 bps per month. The strongest anomalies net, at best, 10 bps after controlling for data mining. Several methods for combining anomalies net around 20 bps." Anomaly portfolios overweight stocks at ~4x the NYSE median spread (67bp vs. 16bp in 2014). Decay increases from ~50% (gross) to **93%** after trading costs.
- Patton & Weller (2020, JFE 137(2):515–549): Based on real implementation costs (as measured by mutual fund returns), typical funds earn low value returns and *no* momentum returns.

**The Retail Subtlety:** For a very small account ($10k-$500k), the *market impact* is negligible — the very cost element that destroys Chen/Velikov/Patton/Weller at institutional size. BUT: Spread (often several percent for micro/nano caps) and relatively high minimum fees/fixed costs dominate. The net size is highly strategy dependent; the academic evidence suggests that the average micro-cap anomaly by spread for retail is close to zero or negative as long as turnover is high. Only very low turnover approaches (buy-and-hold value/quality in micro-caps) have a realistic chance of positive net returns.

**Conclusion B:** The niche exists and is actually uninteresting for institutions (capacity too small). But the claim that it is therefore a *usable* retail edge is mostly a cost-hidden illusion, except when turnover is low.

**C) “Institutional coercive restrictions create exploitable, non-competitive opportunities” — PARTIALLY CONFIRMED, with a differentiated picture**

- **Fire Sales / Forced Selling (CONFIRMED, continues to exist):** Coval & Stafford (2007, JFE 86(2):479-512) show that funds with large outflows create price pressure on their holdings; Investors trading against distressed funds earn significant liquidity premiums, and the forced trades are partially predictable (front-running incentive). Ellul/Jotikasthira/Lundblad (2011, JFE 101(3):596–620) document ratings-driven forced sales of downgraded bonds by insurers with price declines and subsequent reversals — liquidity providers earn abnormal returns, especially when the insurance industry itself is stressed.
- **Index rebalancing (S&P 500 addition effect GONE):** The classic effect (Shleifer 1986; Harris & Gurel 1986; Chen/Noronha/Singal 2004) with historically several percent announcement returns has eroded. Bennett/Stulz/Wang (2023, “Does Joining the S&P 500 Index Hurt Firms?”, 1997–2017): “the positive announcement effect on the stock price of index inclusion has disappeared and the long-run impact of index inclusion has become negative.” Greenwood/Sammon document the decline from 7.4% (1990s) to a statistically insignificant ~1% (2010s); the deletion effect follows the same trend towards zero. However, for *smaller* indices with less AUM and more discretion, the effect may persist.
- **Mutual Fund Flow Price Pressure (CONFIRMED):** Lou (2012, RFS, "A Flow-Based Explanation for Return Predictability") shows that flow-induced trading (funds scaling existing positions with inflows/outflows) creates return predictability.
- **Spin-offs, odd-lot tenders, squeeze-outs:** Continue to exist as special situations (see Part 2).

**Conclusion C:** Confirmed for flow/rating driven fire sales; refuted/weakened for the prominent S&P 500 addition effect. The claim is correct as a category, but "not competed away" only applies where the capacity is small and the trigger is non-informative/coercive.

**D) “Time horizon advantage (enduring drawdowns without redemption risk)” — PARTIALLY CONFIRMED, but practically fragile**

Theoretically well-founded: Shleifer & Vishny (1997, "The Limits of Arbitrage") show that arbitrageurs with foreign capital have to liquidate (redemptions) precisely when opportunities are greatest - those who do not bear the redemption risk have a real structural advantage. Berk & Green (2004, JPE) explain why fund alpha is competed away by flows. A private investor without external investors does not have this structural constraint.

**The opposite position (behavioral):** The evidence shows that private investors typically *do not* realize the staying power advantage. Barber & Odean ("Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth," 2000) show that the most active private traders earn the worst net returns. The “behavioral gap” literature (despite methodological criticism of Dalbar) consistently points to pro-cyclical reallocation that reduces returns. So the theoretical advantage is real, but the *ability* to exploit it is not empirically present for the median retail investor.

**Conclusion D:** Confirmed as a capacity/structure advantage; questionable as a *realized* benefit for the typical retail investor — it is a condition, not a guarantee.

**E) Sharpe Standard Error Formula — CONFIRMED**

The formula SE(SR) ≈ √((1 + SR²/2)/T) is correct and corresponds to Lo (2002, Financial Analysts Journal, “The Statistics of Sharpe Ratios”) for iid normally distributed returns (asymptotic normality: ŜR ~ N(SR, (1 + ½SR²)/T)).

Number check: With true *annualized* SR = 0.4 and T = 5 years, for annual observations: SE ≈ √((1 + 0.08)/5) = √0.216 ≈ 0.465; t = 0.4/0.465 ≈ 0.86. **Confirmed.** For 5% significance (t ≈ 1.96) you need T = (1.96/0.4)² × (1 + 0.08) = 24.01 × 1.08 ≈ 25.9 years. **Confirmed (~26 years).**

Important: The formula only applies under iid + normality. When autocorrelation is positive, the annualized Sharpe is overestimated and the true SE is underestimated (Lo shows overestimates of the annualized Sharpe of up to ~65% for serially correlated hedge fund returns); the correction is made using GMM/Newey-West adjusted standard errors. In case of non-normality (negative skew, excess kurtosis — typical of option/credit strategies), the extended formula (Mertens 2002; Opdyke 2007; Bailey & López de Prado Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio) must be used, which includes skew and kurtosis; negative skew + high kurtosis further increase the SE. The numerical examples in the assertion sentence are therefore correct under their (implicit) assumptions, but the assumptions are often violated for real strategies - usually to the detriment of significance.

**F) “DSR cannot be calculated without a trial count, and this cannot be verified by an individual researcher” — PARTIALLY CONFIRMED, but the criticism overestimates the problem**

The correct thing is: The deflated Sharpe ratio (Bailey & López de Prado 2014, Journal of Portfolio Management 40(5):94–107) requires as inputs: the observed Sharpe, sample length T, skew and kurtosis of the returns as well as the *(effective) number of tested variants N* and the variance of the Sharpe ratios across the trials. The False Strategy Theorem (López de Prado & Bailey 2018) shows that the expected maximum Sharpe grows with N and is "right-unbounded" — without N no exact deflation is possible. For the individual researcher, N is indeed difficult to fix objectively (many informal, correlated experiments).

**But the criticism is not fatal:** (1) N does not have to be the literal trial count — López de Prado proposes the *effective* trial count via clustering correlated strategies (ONC algorithm, hierarchical clustering for a conservative lower bound, eigenvalue/spectral methods). (2) For internal research the following applies: N conservatively *over*estimating is safe. (3) There are alternatives that do not require an explicit trial count or address the problem differently: Harvey & Liu (2015, “Backtesting”, JPM) and Harvey/Liu/Zhu (2016) via multiple testing adjustment; **Romano-Wolf** and **Hansen's SPA (Superior Predictive Ability)** control the Family-Wise Error Rate or test against a benchmark taking into account the entire tested family (but requires knowledge of the family); **PBO/CSCV** (Probability of Backtest Overfitting via Combinatorially Symmetric Cross-Validation, Bailey et al.) measures overfitting data-driven via in-/out-of-sample splits without explicit N-count; True out-of-sample/walk-forward and Bayesian approaches (Jensen/Kelly/Pedersen) partially bypass the trial count.

**Conclusion F:** The claim is correct in the narrow sense (the DSR *needs* N, and N cannot be clearly verified by an individual researcher), but it exaggerates the practical consequence: the overfitting kernel can also be robustly addressed via cross-validation (PBO), real OOS and conservative N upper bounds.

### PART 2 — Where can the edge come from?

For each category: mechanism, best evidence, post-publication status, capacity, net retail revenue, data requirements (<500 USD/month?), hurdles.

**1. Micro/Small/Nano Cap Effects**
Mechanism: Low institutional coverage, limits to arbitrage, illiquidity premium. Evidence: McLean/Pontiff (higher persistence in illiquid stocks); but Chen/Velikov (average ~4 bp/month net, strongest anomalies max. 10 bp). Post-publication: gross existence, net predominantly eroded. Capacity: very small (ideal for retail account size, therefore avoided by institutions). Net retail: close to zero to slightly positive only with low turnover; Spread is the killer. Data: CRSP-like data heavy <$500; SEC EDGAR + fundamental data partly free. Hurdles: spread, liquidity, delisting risk, susceptibility to fraud (pump and dump in the nano-cap area). **Strength of evidence: medium for existence, weak for usable net edge.**

**2. Merger/Risk Arbitrage & Odd Lot Tender**
Mechanism: Spread between offer price and market price to compensate for deal risk (put-like payout profile). Evidence: Mitchell & Pulvino (2001, JF 56(6):2135–2175): 4,750 deals 1963–1998; ~4% annual excess return after costs and contingent claims adjustment; gross ~9.25%, after costs ~3.54% (RAIM, market beta 0.12); Returns are similar to selling naked index puts (correlated in sharply falling markets). Baker & Savasoglu (2002): ~0.8%/month after costs. Post-publication: continues to exist, but spreads have tightened. Capacity: moderate for cash deals in medium-sized targets; Odd lot priority only for small positions (typically <100 shares) → structurally only accessible to retail. Netto Retail: Cash merger spreads after retail fees thin, but positive for diversification; Odd lot tenders can be disproportionate for certain offers (proration-exempt), but are rare and have limited capacity. Data: Deal announcements free (SEC, press releases); specialized trackers sometimes <500 USD. Hurdles: deal break risk (tail), legal subtleties in tender conditions. **Strength of evidence: strong (top journal), but returns moderate and tail-biased.**

**3. Event-driven special situations (spin-offs, rights issues, liquidations, SPAC redemptions, squeeze-outs/judgment proceedings)**
Mechanism: Forced/mechanical sellers (index funds sell spin-off), information inertia, structural mispricing. SPAC Redemption: Right to redeem shares at escrow value (~$10 + interest) while retaining the warrant/upside — almost low-risk "SPAC arbitrage" as long as you exit before the redemption deadline. German/EU context: According to a squeeze-out/control and profit transfer agreement, minority shareholders have subsequent improvement rights; In the adjudication process, the severance payment is reviewed in court and often improved (with statutory interest) - a niche with structural capacity limitations and largely avoided institutionally. Evidence: Post-spin-off drift historically documented; SPAC arbitrage 2020-2021 well documented (practical/working paper). Post-publication: spin-off drift weakened; SPAC redemption mechanics continue to exist in terms of regulation, but with less free lunch after 2022. Capacity: small to moderate. Netto Retail: SPAC redemption arb was real positive with low risk in the boom phase; Real returns from adjudication proceedings, but with a very long capital commitment (years) and litigation risk. Data: SEC EDGAR (SPAC prospectuses), Federal Gazette (DE adjudication proceedings) — free. Hurdles: very long holding periods, legal complexity, low liquidity. **Level of evidence: mixed to strong depending on subcategory; SPAC/appraisal process practitioner/working paper evidenced.**

**4. Forced flow (index exclusion of small indices, fallen angels, tax loss selling/January effect)**
Mechanism like C. Fallen Angels: Rating forced sales (Ellul et al.) → price decline + reversal. Tax-loss selling: selling pressure among annual losers in December, recovery in January (especially micro-caps). Post-Publication: S&P 500 addition effect dead (Bennett/Stulz/Wang); The fallen angel effect still exists (especially bonds); January effect has largely disappeared in large caps, partly persistent in illiquid micro caps, but questionable on a net basis according to the spread. Capacity: small. Netto Retail: Fall angel bonds difficult to trade for retail (minimum denomination, OTC spreads); Tax loss bounce in micro caps spread limited. Data: rating campaigns, calendar — partly free. **Level of evidence: medium (Fallen Angels strong, January effect weak/eroded).**

**5. Illiquidity/Patience Premiums & Closed-End Fund Discounts**
Mechanism: Amihud (2002) — illiquidity premium; CEF discount mean reversion. Evidence: Pontiff (1995, JFE 37; 1996 QJE “Costly Arbitrage: Evidence from Closed-End Funds”): CEF discounts predict returns but are not arbitraged away because of arbitrage costs (idiosyncratic risk); Discounts are highly persistent. Activist arbitrage (open-ending) can close discounts (Bradley/Brav/Goldstein/Jiang). Post-Publication: CEF discount predictability continues to exist (limits to arbitrage). Capacity: CEF market small → ideal retail. Netto Retail: CEF discount purchase + hedge real, but hedge difficult for retail; Pure long discount approach carries market risk. Data: CEF-NAVs/Discounts partly free (e.g. CEFConnect). Hurdles: idiosyncratic risk, hedging costs. **Strength of evidence: strong for existence/persistence, medium for usable net edge.**

**6. Volatility risk premium / option sales**
Mechanism: Options/variance buyers pay a premium for protection against volatility/crash risk; On average, sellers earn this premium. Evidence (strong, top journals): Coval & Shumway (2001, JF 56(3):983–1009): “zero-beta, at-the-money straddle positions produce average losses of approximately three percent per week” (⇒ sellers win gross). Bakshi & Kapadia (2003, RFS 16(2):527–566): Delta-hedged gains are systematically negative — “supportive of a negative market volatility risk premium”. Carr & Wu (2009, RFS 22(3):1311–1341): Short variance Sharpe ~0.85–0.98 (S&P 500/100/Dow) — well above stocks; BUT with synthetic swap rates, gross. Bondarenko (2014, Quarterly Journal of Finance): “overpriced puts puzzle” cannot be explained by standard models. Post-Publication: VRP continues to exist (fundamental risk premium, not a pure anomaly bug). Tail risk: CBOE PutWrite (PUT) max drawdown ~−33% (vs. S&P −51%), but pronounced negative skew; Volmageddon (Feb 5, 2018): VIX jumped from 17.31 to 37.32, inverse vol ETPs lost >90% in a day (XIV fell from ~$108 to ~$4 and was liquidated), while the S&P 500 only gained -4.1% — illustration of the ruin risk of leveraged short vol positions. Netto Retail: Retail options traders lose on average — Bryzgalova/Pavlova/Sikorskaya (2023, JF 78(6):3465–3514): average bid-ask spread of 12.6% for weekly options preferred by retail, “lose money on average”; de Silva/Smith/So (2025, JFE): Retail losses 5-9% per trade (10-14% for high-vol announcements). BUT: The loss primarily affects option *buyers*; Using trader-level data, Bogousslavsky & Muravyev (2024, Working Paper) find that naked option sales earn ~+20% on average (consistent with VRP) and that total losses (−0.9%/trade) are smaller than aggregate proxies suggest. Retail margin and assignment risk are real hurdles. Data: Option prices partly free/cheap (<500 USD). Hurdles: tail risk (risk of ruin with naked positions), margin calls, discipline. **Level of evidence: very strong for the existence of the VRP; mixed/conflicting for retail net profitability; Tail risk real and potentially ruinous.**

**7. Seasonalities/calendar effects**
Survived: FOMC cycle effect (Cieslak/Morse/Vissing-Jorgensen 2019, JF 74(5):2201–2248): "since 1994, the equity premium is earned entirely in weeks 0, 2, 4, and 6 in Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) cycle time" (even weeks from the last FOMC meeting); robust across subperiods and internationally; also pre-FOMC announcement drift (Lucca/Moench ~0.5% over 24h). Turn-of-the-month robustly documented. Halloween/Sell-in-May controversial (partly suspected of data mining, partly confirmed out of sample). Dead/eroded: January effect in large caps, many intraday patterns after HFT. Post-Publication: FOMC effect robust so far, but be careful about possible arbitrage. Capacity: high (index level, liquid). Netto Retail: FOMC/ToM timing can be traded with liquid ETFs, low costs; Edge small and volatile. Dates: Calendar free. Hurdles: timing risk, possible disappearance. **Level of evidence: strong for FOMC/ToM (top journal), weak for Halloween/Intraday.**

**8. Crypto & Non-Stock Niches (Funding Rate/Basic Trades)**
Mechanism: Perpetual futures funding rate keeps perp price at spot; Cash-and-carry (long spot, short perp) collects positive funding payments in a market-neutral manner. Evidence: He et al. (2023) — Perp prices deviate 60-100% annualized from spot (much more than in TradFi); MDPI study (2025, 35.7 million observations, 26 exchanges): 17% of observations with ≥20 bp spread, but only 40% of top opportunities net profitable after costs/reversals. Post-publication: continues to exist (structural, retail-driven demand). Capacity: small to moderate, retail accessible. Netto Retail: real positive in high funding phases, but counterparty/exchange risk (FTX collapse), smart contract risk (DeFi), liquidation/margin risk, regulatory uncertainty. Data: on-chain/exchange APIs mostly free. Hurdles: operational and counterparty risk dominate. **Level of evidence: mixed (predominantly working papers/practitioners), mechanism solid, risks significant.**

**9. Frontier/Small Peripheral Markets**
Mechanism: low institutional coverage → more mispricing. Evidence: Jacobs/Müller (2020, JFE) — internationally *no* reliable post-publication decrease (anomalies more persistent outside the USA). Post-publication: more persistent than in the USA. Capacity: small. Netto Retail: access/FX/custody costs and some capital controls; Spreads high. Data: heavy/expensive. Hurdles: access, regulation, governance risk. **Level of evidence: medium for persistence, weak for retail accessibility.**

**10. Cost/Tax/Structural Arbitrage**
Mechanism: No signal alpha, but deterministic savings/yield. Tax loss harvesting, fee optimization, securities lending income, favorable margin rates. Evidence: Tax loss harvesting value quantified in the literature (working paper/practitioner, magnitude strongly dependent on jurisdiction, tax rate and volatility). Post-publication: structural, does not disappear. Capacity: scales with portfolio. Netto Retail: the *most reliable* contribution ever because it is deterministic instead of probabilistic. Data: own account details. Hurdles: tax law (in Germany: loss offsetting pots, partial exemption, advance flat rate; in the USA: wash sale rules). **Level of evidence: strong/deterministic but limited in magnitude.**

**11. Alternative data sources (EDGAR, Form 4, 13F/13D, Short Interest, FTD)**
Mechanism: Public, but processed unequally. Insider purchases (Form 4) — especially “opportunistic” insiders — predict returns (Cohen/Malloy/Pomorski 2012, JF “Decoding Inside Information”); 13D filings (activists) generate positive announcement returns (Brav et al.). Post-Publication: Insider signal continues to exist, but weakened and quickly priced in. Capacity: moderate. Netto Retail: signal real, but limit latency (institutions read EDGAR faster) and implementation costs; May be usable for small accounts in small caps. Data: EDGAR Full-Text, Form 4, 13F, Short Interest, FTD predominantly *free* → fits <500 USD. Hurdles: processing/NLP effort (AI agents helpful here), latency. **Level of evidence: strong for insider/13D existence, medium for retail net edge.**

**12. Non-forecasting approaches (market making, rebalancing premium, portfolio construction)**
Mechanism: liquidity provision instead of forecast; rebalancing/diversification premium; Risk parity. Evidence: Market making in illiquid instruments earns spread, but competes with HFT (for retail only conceivable in very illiquid niches without HFT interest). Rebalancing premium ("volatility harvesting") theoretically proven, but small in magnitude and regime-dependent. Post-publication: structural. Capacity: varies. Netto Retail: Market making for retail is usually not competitive; Systematic, low-cost portfolio construction (broad diversification, disciplined rebalancing) is the most solid "contribution" — but it is beta harvest, not alpha edge. Data: free. Hurdles: HFT competition in market making. **Strength of evidence: weak for market making retail edge, solid for portfolio construction as beta optimization.**

### Ranking by evidence level (for usable retail net edge)

1. **Cost/Tax/Structural Arbitrage (Cat. 10):** Deterministic, reliable, but limited in size. Highest base rate for positive net contribution.
2. **Volatility Risk Premium as a Seller (Cat. 6):** Strongest academic evidence for existence; but real ruin/tail risk and conflicting retail net evidence. Only with strict risk management.
3. **Merger Arb / Odd Lot Tender / SPAC Redemption / Judgment Proceedings (Cat. 2, 3):** Top journal evidence for moderate positive net returns; Capacity limited (i.e. not out of competition), but subject to deal/tail/time risk.
4. **Forced-Flow / Fire Sales / Fallen Angels (Cat. 3, 4):** Strong evidence for existence; sometimes difficult to implement for retail (bond access).
5. **CEF discounts / illiquidity premium (Cat. 5):** Strong persistence evidence, but hedging/idiosyncratic risk.
6. **FOMC/Turn-of-the-Month seasonality (Cat. 7):** Top journal, liquid tradable, but small and potentially disappearing edge.
7. **Alternative data / insider signals (Cat. 11):** Real signals, but latency/cost disadvantage compared to Institutions.
8. **Crypto Funding/Basic (Cat. 8):** Mechanism solid, but predominantly non-peer-reviewed and with significant counterparty risks.
9. **Micro/Nano Cap Anomalies (Cat. 1):** Existence medium, usable net edge weak (spread).
10. **Frontier markets (Cat. 9):** Persistence medium, retail access weak.
11. **Market-Making-Retail (Cat. 12):** Weakest evidence for retail edge.

### Base rate: How likely is a usable net edge?

The hard counter-evidence: Barber/Lee/Liu/Odean (Taiwan data, "Do Individual Day Traders Make Money?", TWSE 1995-1999, >130,000 investors): "More than eight out of ten day traders lose money" — only about 20% make any net profit at all in a 6-month window, and the *persistent* profitable ones are a small minority. "The Cross-Section of Speculator Skill" (Barber/Lee/Liu/Odean, JFE, 1992-2006) confirms: "Few day traders are able to earn positive abnormal returns net of fees" — profitable day traders are consistently around 5% of active traders, and only ~1% are reliably profitable over time. Barber/Huang/Odean/Schwarz (2022, JF 77(6):3141–3190, Robinhood): Attention-driven herd behavior predicts *negative* returns — “Average 20-day abnormal returns are −4.7% for the top stocks purchased each day” (up to −9% in extreme episodes). Barber & Odean (2000): the most active traders achieve the worst net returns.

**Realistic Base Rate:** For a novice programmer with basic stats, small account, no HFT/Prime brokerage, the probability of finding a cost-repeatable net edge in one of the *forecast-based* signal categories is **low (roughly in the low single-digit percentage range, consistent with the ~1% persistently profitable day trader and ~5% intermittently profitable)**. The probability is significantly higher in *non-forecast-based* or *structural* categories: cost/tax optimization (almost certainly positive contribution, but small), capacity-limited event arbitrage (moderate opportunity, but operationally demanding and tail-affected) and VRP sales with strict risk management (positive expectation, but risk of ruin).

## Recommendations

**Level 1 (immediate, highest base rate, deterministic):** Focus on structural/cost/tax optimization (Cat. 10) and low-cost, broadly diversified portfolio construction with disciplined rebalancing (Cat. 12). This is not an “alpha edge”, but the most reliable, immediately realizable net contribution. Benchmark for moving forward: only when this basis is in place.

**Level 2 (according to a solid statistical/programming basis):** Check *capacity-limited event situations* with free data - odd lot tender, SPAC redemption mechanics, in the DE/EU area adjudication process/remedial action (Cat. 2, 3). These are institutionally avoided (too small) and therefore “not outcompeted”, but require legal care and patience (capital commitment over years). Threshold: only with position sizes that can handle the total loss of individual deals (diversification across many deals).

**Level 3 (only with strict risk management):** VRP sales (Cat. 6) in a small, defined-risk form (e.g. covered/defined spreads instead of naked options), with a hard position limit against tail ruin. Termination criterion: If the realized skew/drawdown only approaches the Volmageddon magnitude, pause the strategy.

**Consistent - Methodology against self-deception:** Harden every signaling strategy against overfitting *before* investing capital: true out-of-sample/walk-forward, PBO/CSCV, deflated Sharpe with conservatively *over*estimated N, and internalize the insight from E): A true annualized Sharpe of 0.4 takes ~26 years for 5% significance - i.e. h. short backtests can almost never reliably separate real small edges from noise. Benchmark that would change the recommendation: documented, true-to-cost live net performance over multiple years that is statistically separable from noise.

## Caveats
- Many crypto, tax harvesting and SPAC numbers come from working papers/practitioner sources, not from top journals — considered weaker evidence.
- The CBOE PutWrite numbers and Carr/Wu Sharpe ratios are *gross* (Carr/Wu use synthetic swap rates); Real retail net returns are significantly lower due to spreads. The Bondarenko-PutWrite studies are CBOE sponsored (potential sponsor bias).
- The retail option loss evidence (Bryzgalova; de Silva) and the opposite Bogousslavsky/Muravyev evidence contradict each other; The resolution lies in the buyer/seller distinction (buyers lose, sellers collect the VRP).
- German/EU adjudication returns are highly dependent on individual cases and the law; no generalizable magnitude.
- Note time frame: Many anomaly studies end before 2015; the post-2015 net erosion (Chen/Velikov) could be even stronger.
- This is purely a presentation of evidence, not investment advice and not a promise of returns.
